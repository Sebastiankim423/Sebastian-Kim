# <Engagement> — Engagement Brief

## The Problem 

The farm owns a small 1.5-acre market garden with 64 beds and limited labor. Before the season starts, the farm must decide how many beds to dedicate to tomatoes, carrots, and mesclun. Once those decisions are made, they cannot be changed during the season. The goal is to make the most profit while staying within the limits on land and labor. The decision is difficult because planting more of a crop makes it less efficient and more expensive to manage. The farm is trying to find the best mix of crops that earns the most money without exceeding resources.

## What is Fixed, What Can Be Chosen, and What Limits the Choice? 

The market prices, production costs, season length, available labor, total beds, and crop bed caps are all fixed. The only factor the farmer can choose is how many beds to dedicate to tomatoes, carrots, and mesclun. The goal is to find the right combination of crops that will maximize profits. The farmer's decisions are limited by the amount of land, labor, and crop space available. The farm has only 64 beds, each crop has a maximum number of beds that can be planted, and labor hours are limited. Costs also increase as more beds of the same crop are planted due to diminishing returns.

## What I Am Assuming

I am assuming that the prices, labor, fertilizer cost, and diminishing return rates are correct and will stay the same for the season. I am also assuming that all the crops produced can be sold at the listed prices. 

If I had more time, I would test how much labor limits and diminishing returns affect the profit, since either one could change the best crop mix.

## Hypothesis

For my hypothesis, I would say the farm should use all 20 tomato beds, with most of the remaining beds going to mesclun and fewer to carrots.

## Reasoning

Tomatoes should use all 20 available beds because they generate the highest revenue per bed ($8,800), making them the most profitable crop at lower production levels. After using the tomatoes, I suggest most of the remaining beds to be planted with mesclun because it generates $2,700 per bed, compared to $2,094 per bed for carrots. Mesclun also has the lowest diminishing return rate (1.25%) compared to carrots (2.5%) and tomatoes (10%), so the costs would increase more slowly as more beds are planted. 

## How I Would Know I Was Wrong

I would be wrong if the model shows that using fewer than 20 tomato beds or more carrot beds would result in higher profits. I would also be wrong if labor limits or increasing costs make a different crop mix more profitable than my tomato-heavy plan.
