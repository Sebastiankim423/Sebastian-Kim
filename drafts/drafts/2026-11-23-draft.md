## Introduction
For decades, NBA teams sold local broadcast rights to regional sports networks (RSNs). The networks paid for those rights with advertising and with monthly fees that cable companies collected from every cable household, whether or not the household watched sports. As consumers cut the cord, that model failed: Forbes estimates that NBA teams lost about $453 million in local media revenue between 2023-24 and 2025-26, as average local television revenue fell from about $48 million to $33 million per team (Chierchio, 2026), and CNBC names Main Street Sports, formerly Diamond Sports Group, as a visible casualty (CNBC, 2026). Interest in basketball remains strong, so this paper argues that the collapse of the cable-bundle model that financed local broadcasting, not a loss of fan interest, explains the lost revenue, and that the NBA should respond with a centralized streaming strategy.
## Supply and Demand
The concept doing the most work in this paper is the difference between a movement along a curve and a shift of the curve. Streaming gave households substitutes for cable, so demand for cable television shifted left from D1 to D2 in Figure 2. A leftward demand shift alone lowers both the equilibrium price and the equilibrium quantity. Quantity did fall (Figure 1), but price did not. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) price index for cable, satellite, and live streaming television service rose from 427.5 in January 2016 to 614.8 in September 2026, or 43.8% (Figure 3). Higher prices together with fewer subscribers cannot come from a demand shift alone. They require a leftward shift in supply large enough to outweigh the fall in price that the demand shift causes.
That supply shift comes from carriage fees, the fees networks charge cable and satellite companies to carry their channels. Sports networks charged distributors more over this period (Axios, 2022). Because the fee is a cost of assembling the bundle, it sits behind the supply curve: higher fees shift supply left from S1 to S2, and distributors passed them on to households. At the new equilibrium E2 in Figure 2, subscribers fall from Q1 to Q2 and the price rises from P1 to P2.
Two cautions apply to the price evidence. First, prices in general rose too. The all-items consumer price index went from 236.9 to 336.9 over the same months, up 42.2% (BLS, 2026), so television service rose only about one percent faster than prices in general. Most of the nominal increase is inflation: the real price stayed near flat while quantity fell sharply, as offsetting shifts would produce. Second, the series does not price the cable bundle alone. BLS defines it as subscriptions to cable, satellite, and live-streaming television and excludes prerecorded on-demand services (BLS, n.d.); "live streaming" entered the series title only in February 2023 (BLS, 2023).
## Elasticity
Elasticity adds less. Substitutes make demand more elastic, so streaming suggests cable demand became more price-responsive, but my data do not show it. Subscribers fell while the real price barely moved, which identifies a shift in demand, not a change in its slope.
## Consumer Choice
Consumer choice explains why households left. A household maximizes utility within a budget, and the bundle charges it for dozens of channels it rarely watches. Streaming lets households buy only what they value, so many conclude that the bundle’s opportunity cost is too high. Fans kept watching basketball; they stopped paying through the bundle.
## Market Structure and Pricing Power
Market structure explains why the bundle could finance local games. An RSN typically held exclusive local rights, giving it bargaining power over distributors. Because the bundle charged every household, non-fans cross-subsidized fans: the network collected fees from households that never watched a game and could therefore pay teams large rights fees. Substitutes now limit what networks can charge, and a network with fewer subscribers cannot honor contracts signed when the bundle was large.
## Proposed Strategy and Defense
The NBA should aggregate local rights at the league level and sell games through one direct-to-consumer streaming service, supplemented by free over-the-air broadcasts in selected markets. This matches how fans now buy and ends the league’s dependence on failing RSNs. Forbes reports the league is exploring this (Chierchio, 2026).
The main objection is that streaming cannot replace the bundle, because only fans pay. Replacing the $33 million in average local revenue at $20 a month takes about 137,500 subscribers for a full year ($20 × 12 × 137,500). The price is realistic: the Lakers’ Spectrum SportsNet+ service costs $19.99 a month (Awful Announcing, n.d.-b). The bundle charged every cable household in a team’s territory, so a team whose network reached 1.5 million households would need about 9% of them to subscribe, and one reaching 1 million would need almost 14%, before platform costs.
## Alternative Explanation and Falsifiability
	The argument would be weakened if fan interest were falling. In 2024-25, national NBA viewership fell only 2%, while local viewership fell 13% across the 25 teams with data, which the report attributes to cord-cutting and pricier RSN tiers; ratings also miss paid streaming (Awful Announcing, n.d.-a). Stable national interest alongside shrinking local audiences supports the argument, though local ratings are an imperfect test.
## Conclusion
The collapse of the regional sports network model is a revenue problem, not a popularity problem. Cord-cutting shrank the subscription base on which RSNs depended, ending the cross-subsidy that paid for local rights. A centralized streaming platform with free broadcast partners is the most promising response.
